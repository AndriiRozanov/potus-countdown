HOW IS THE U.S. PRESIDENT ELECTED?

Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer

The U.S. president is not elected directly by national popular vote. Instead, voters in each state choose electors who make up the Electoral College, and a candidate wins by securing at least 270 of the 538 electoral votes. The full process runs from primaries and caucuses in the spring, to party conventions in summer, to the general election in November, to the Electoral College vote in December, and finally Inauguration Day on January 20.

Who can run for president

Before the process even begins, a candidate must meet three requirements set by the Constitution:

  • Be a natural-born U.S. citizen.
  • Be at least 35 years old.
  • Have lived in the United States for at least 14 years.

Anyone who meets these three can run. The 22nd Amendment adds one more limit: no person can be elected president more than twice.

Step 1: Primaries and caucuses

In the first half of an election year, each party decides who its nominee will be. States hold primaries (secret-ballot elections) or caucuses (local meetings where voters gather to show support). These contests award delegates to the candidates.

The goal for each candidate is to collect a majority of their party's delegates. The winner becomes that party's presumptive nominee, usually well before the primaries even finish.

Step 2: National conventions

In the summer, each party holds a national convention where the delegates formally nominate the candidate for president. The nominee also announces a running mate — the candidate for vice president. After the conventions, the race narrows to the two major-party nominees (plus any independent or third-party candidates) heading into the general election.

Step 3: The general election

The general election is held every four years on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. The next one is in November 2028. On that day — and in early voting and mail ballots before it — Americans cast their votes.

Here is the key point that surprises many people: when you vote for a presidential candidate, you are really voting for a slate of electors pledged to that candidate in your state. The national popular vote total does not directly decide the winner. What matters is who wins each state.

Step 4: The Electoral College

The Electoral College is the system that actually elects the president. There are 538 electors in total, and a candidate needs a majority — 270 — to win.

Each state's number of electors equals its representation in Congress:

  • Two electors for its two U.S. senators, plus
  • One elector for each U.S. House seat (which depends on population).
  • Washington, D.C. gets 3 electors, even though it has no voting members of Congress.

So California, the most populous state, has 54 electoral votes, while small states like Wyoming have just 3. In 48 states and D.C., whoever wins the state's popular vote takes all of its electoral votes — a "winner-take-all" rule. Only Maine and Nebraska split their electors by congressional district.

This is why a handful of closely divided "swing states" decide most elections: candidates focus there because that is where the electoral votes are actually up for grabs. For a deeper look, see our guide to what the Electoral College is and how it works.

Why you can win the vote but lose the election

Because the winner is decided by electoral votes rather than the national popular vote, it is possible to win the most votes nationwide and still lose the presidency. This has happened five times in American history, most recently in 2000 (Bush over Gore) and 2016 (Trump over Clinton). It is one of the most debated features of the U.S. system.

Step 5: Counting and certifying the result

Winning on election night is the practical result, but several formal steps follow:

  • December: the electors meet in their states and cast their official electoral votes.
  • January 6: Congress meets in a joint session to count the electoral votes and certify the winner.
  • If no one reaches 270: the election goes to the House of Representatives in a contingent election, where each state delegation casts one vote and 26 states are needed to win. This last happened in 1824.

Step 6: Inauguration Day

The final step is the inauguration. Under the 20th Amendment, the new presidential term begins at noon on January 20. The president-elect takes the oath of office and the term officially begins. It is the same rule that fixes when Trump's current term ends — January 20, 2029.

The process at a glance

  • Spring: primaries and caucuses pick each party's nominee.
  • Summer: national conventions make it official and name running mates.
  • November: the general election — voters choose electors state by state.
  • December: the Electoral College casts its votes.
  • January 6: Congress counts and certifies.
  • January 20: Inauguration Day — the new term begins.

Frequently asked questions

How is the U.S. president elected?

Not by direct national popular vote. Voters in each state choose electors who form the Electoral College, and a candidate wins with at least 270 of 538 electoral votes. The process runs from primaries to conventions to the November election to the Electoral College vote to Inauguration Day.

What is the Electoral College and how many votes are needed to win?

It is the group of 538 electors that formally elects the president. Each state gets electors equal to its senators plus House seats, and D.C. gets three. A candidate needs a majority — 270 — to win.

Can you become president without winning the popular vote?

Yes. Because electoral votes decide the winner, a candidate can win the presidency while losing the national popular vote. It has happened five times, most recently in 2000 and 2016.

When is the U.S. presidential election held?

Every four years on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. The next one is in November 2028, with inauguration the following January 20.

What happens if no candidate gets 270 electoral votes?

The election goes to the House, which picks the president in a contingent election. Each state delegation casts one vote, and 26 states are needed to win. This last happened in 1824.
